projects/continental/README.md ● live

Continental Dry Cleaners

A production website for a real Nairobi dry-cleaning business: collection and on-site cleaning across the city, explained clearly enough that a customer on a phone can go from “how much?” to a booked pickup in a few taps.

role
Design, front-end engineering, QA, deployment
type
Client project · live in production
stack
React 19 · TypeScript · GSAP · Vercel
links
continental-dry-cleaners.vercel.app ↗

Context

Continental is a working business, not a concept. Its customers arrive with practical questions: what do you clean, what does it cost, do you come to me, and how do I book? The site had to answer them clearly, then hand the customer straight to a conversation with the business.

What I built

  • A mobile-first service experience. The layout is designed for phones first and scales up to desktop, because that is where customers arrive.
  • Transparent pricing. Prices are shown up front, so customers can decide before they reach out. Not knowing the cost is one of the most common reasons people don’t enquire at all.
  • Guided WhatsApp conversion. WhatsApp is where this business already talks to customers, so the site works with it instead of replacing it. The flow guides a visitor towards a WhatsApp conversation with the business, keeping the step from interest to enquiry short.
  • Collection and on-site cleaning. Both ways of working with Continental across Nairobi are explained on the site.
  • Theme support. Light and dark themes.
  • Considered motion. GSAP animation adds polish without getting in the way of the content.

Engineering decisions

React 19 with TypeScript. Typed service and pricing data keep the catalogue consistent as it grows, and catch mistakes at build time rather than in front of a customer.

Vercel for hosting. Fast global delivery, HTTPS by default, and simple deploys for a site a small business depends on.

Quality

Because real customers use it, the site went through production QA before launch: checking content and prices, testing the conversion flow end to end, and reviewing layout, readability, and interaction across screen sizes and both themes.

What I learned

Shipping for a real business changes what “done” means. The measure is not how the site looks in a portfolio but whether the owner gets better enquiries. Meeting customers where they already are (phones, WhatsApp, clear prices) mattered more than any single feature.

projects/doc-platform/README.md distributed ai

AI document platform

A document management platform built as a production-style microservice system. Users upload PDFs, Word files, or scans; the platform extracts the text, processes it in the background with AI, and makes everything searchable by meaning rather than keywords.

role
Architecture, back-end services, CI/CD, observability
type
Cloud-native systems project
stack
FastAPI · PostgreSQL · RabbitMQ · Celery · Redis · Docker
links
Source on GitHub ↗

Goal

I wanted one project that covered the whole lifecycle of a cloud-native system, not just a single service: distributed communication, background processing, CI/CD, monitoring, security, and AI. Every one of those concerns had to work in practice, with tests and dashboards to prove it.

How it works

A user registers and logs in through the gateway, then creates a document or uploads a file. Text is extracted automatically (pdfplumber for PDFs, Tesseract OCR for images). The document service saves it and publishes a document.created event to RabbitMQ; the notification service picks that up and records a notification for the user. In parallel, a Celery worker generates embeddings and extracts entities, a category, and sentiment.

client ─► api-gateway :8080   JWT · rate limit · security headers · circuit breaker
              ├─► document-service   CRUD · upload/OCR · summarise · semantic search
              │        └─ publishes document.created ─► RabbitMQ ─► notification-service
              ├─► auth-service       register · login · JWT issue/refresh
              └─► notification-service

celery worker (Redis broker)   embeddings · entities · category · sentiment
PostgreSQL (3 databases) · Redis (rate limits, AI cache) · Prometheus + Grafana

The services

  • API gateway. The single public entry point. It validates JWTs, rate-limits by role through Redis (10, 100, or 1,000 requests a minute for guests, users, and admins), adds security headers, tags every request with a correlation ID, and uses a circuit breaker so one failing service doesn’t take the rest down.
  • Auth service. Registration and login with bcrypt-hashed passwords; short-lived access tokens (30 minutes) and longer refresh tokens (7 days).
  • Document service. CRUD, file upload with text extraction, AI summaries, tag suggestions, and semantic search, with documents scoped to their owner.
  • Notification service. Consumes RabbitMQ events so notifications never slow down a user’s request.

AI that degrades gracefully

Every AI feature has a fallback, so the core platform works with no OpenAI key at all. Summaries use GPT-4o-mini, or fall back to an extractive summary. Semantic search uses text-embedding-3-small vectors, or falls back to TF-IDF cosine similarity. Tag suggestions, entity extraction, and sentiment are skipped cleanly rather than failing. AI results are cached in Redis for an hour to keep costs and latency down.

Testing, delivery, and observability

  • Tests on every service, with an 80% coverage floor enforced: document 80%, auth 86%, notification 83%, gateway 82%.
  • GitHub Actions runs the four test suites as a matrix on every push and pull request, then builds and pushes tagged Docker images.
  • Prometheus and Grafana scrape every service’s /metrics; an auto-provisioned dashboard shows request rate, error rate, P95/P99 latency, and health. OpenTelemetry tracing runs on every service.
  • A Typer CLI for logging in, creating, listing, summarising, and searching documents from the terminal.

What I learned

Splitting a system into services is the easy part; making it observable and resilient is the real work. Designing the fallbacks first made the AI features feel dependable instead of fragile, which is the same principle I apply to product work.

projects/medassist-ai/README.md health tech

MedAssist AI

A Kenya-first health platform that combines conservative AI symptom triage with the next step a patient actually needs: finding a vetted clinic, booking an appointment, or ordering over-the-counter medicine, priced in shillings. Clinics get a management dashboard on the other side.

role
Full-stack: product, API, front end, infrastructure
type
Health-tech product
stack
React 19 · TypeScript · FastAPI · PostgreSQL · Docker · AWS
links
Source on GitHub ↗

The problem

Symptom checkers usually stop at advice, and clinic directories usually start with no context. A patient in Nairobi with a worrying symptom needs both: an honest sense of how urgent it is, then a clear path to care they can reach and afford. MedAssist joins those steps in one flow.

What it does

  • AI triage. A short symptom assessment returns a severity level (mild, moderate, or urgent) in under a minute. The logic is deliberately conservative: when it is uncertain, it escalates to professional care, and urgent results surface Kenya’s emergency line.
  • Clinic discovery and booking. Patients compare vetted clinics, see their doctors, and book from real available slots. The seed data covers 13 clinics.
  • Medicine ordering. An over-the-counter catalogue priced in KES, with delivery.
  • Clinic dashboard. Clinic admins manage appointment status and see revenue and trend analytics; subscription tiers make it a SaaS product for clinics.

Architecture

React 19 SPA (Vercel CDN)
        │ /api/*
        ▼
Nginx reverse proxy (SSL via Let's Encrypt)
        ▼
FastAPI backend (Docker)  routes: auth · triage · clinics · appointments · orders · dashboard
        ▼
PostgreSQL 15 (JSONB for medical data) · planned on AWS RDS af-south-1

The back end separates routes, Pydantic schemas, SQLAlchemy models, and a services layer that holds the business logic (the triage engine, booking, and analytics). The front end uses Zustand for authentication state, TanStack Query for server state and caching, React Hook Form with Zod for validated forms, and Recharts for clinic analytics.

Roles and security

There are three roles: patient, clinic admin, and super admin. Every API route declares which role it needs. Authentication uses JWTs signed with python-jose, passwords are hashed with bcrypt, and allowed origins are configured per environment. The deployment plan includes M-Pesa (Safaricom Daraja) credentials for local payments.

Built for Kenyan networks

I set performance targets for 3G rather than office Wi-Fi: under 3 seconds for the landing page and dashboard, under 2 seconds for a triage result, and under 500 ms time-to-first-byte. Vite code-splitting, CDN edge caching, query caching, async FastAPI handlers, and gzip at Nginx are how it gets there.

Delivery

Docker Compose runs the database, API, front end, and Nginx locally with one command, plus a hot-reload dev mode. GitHub Actions handles CI/CD; there is a backend smoke test, a TypeScript type check, and a production build check, plus a written deployment checklist and SSL renewal runbook.

Responsibility

MedAssist is a triage and guidance tool, not a diagnostic or prescribing service. Designing for that honestly (conservative defaults, clear escalation, no false certainty) was the most important product decision in the project.

projects/water-potability/README.md ml study

Water potability

A machine-learning study that asks whether water is safe to drink based only on measurable chemistry, comparing three classifiers on 3,276 samples and being honest about what the data can and can’t tell us.

role
Data analysis, modelling, evaluation, demo app
type
Applied ML study
stack
Python · pandas · scikit-learn · Jupyter · Streamlit
links
Notebook on GitHub ↗

Question

Can a model judge whether water is potable from nine physicochemical measurements: pH, hardness, dissolved solids, chloramines, sulfate, conductivity, organic carbon, trihalomethanes, and turbidity? The target is binary: safe (1) or not safe (0).

Method

  • Cleaning. Several features have missing values. I filled them with the median, which is robust to the skew and outliers in this data.
  • Split. 80% training and 20% test, with a fixed random seed so the results are reproducible.
  • Scaling. Features are standardised for the linear model; tree models use the raw values.
  • Models. Logistic regression as the baseline, then random forest and gradient boosting.

Results

modeltest accuracy
Logistic regression62.8%
Random forest67.4%
Gradient boosting66.9%

Random forest performed best. Feature importance ranked sulfate, pH, and hardness highest, but the scores were close across all nine features. That points to potability depending on interactions between indicators rather than any single threshold, which also explains why a linear model struggles.

Reading the numbers honestly

67% is a real improvement over the linear baseline, but it is not good enough to decide whether someone drinks a glass of water. The point of the study is the evaluation itself: knowing where the model’s ceiling is with this data, and why. In a real deployment, a model like this would flag samples for lab testing, never replace it.

Demo app

A small Streamlit app loads the trained model, reports its test accuracy, and lets you enter the nine measurements to get a prediction, so the study can be explored without opening the notebook.

Next steps

Hyperparameter tuning, k-fold cross-validation instead of a single split, and handling class imbalance (for example with SMOTE) are the clear next experiments, along with reporting precision and recall for the “safe” class, which is the costly one to get wrong.

SafariGo

An intercity bus booking product for Kenya, built around the moment that matters most to a traveller: knowing the seat they picked is really theirs.

role
Product design and engineering
type
Product in progress · private repository
stack
TypeScript
links
Source is private. Happy to walk through it on a call.

Why this product

Booking intercity travel in Kenya, say from Nairobi to Mombasa, can still mean phone calls, queues, and uncertainty about which seat you actually have. SafariGo is my attempt at a booking flow that feels as certain as boarding the bus.

The flow

  • Route discovery. Pick an origin, a destination, and a date, then compare departures.
  • Real-time seat state. A seat map that reflects what is actually available, so a traveller can trust the seat they choose.
  • Passenger details. A short, mobile-friendly form for the details an operator needs.
  • Confirmation. A clear summary of route, time, and seat that a traveller can show at boarding.

Design priorities

Most bookings will happen on a phone, often on the move, so the seat map, forms, and confirmation are designed for small screens and patchy connections first. The hardest engineering problem is seat state: holding a seat long enough to finish paying without locking it forever when someone abandons the booking.

Status

SafariGo is in active development in a private repository. I’m happy to walk through the product and code in an interview.
